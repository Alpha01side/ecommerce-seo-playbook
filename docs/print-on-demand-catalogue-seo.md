# Print-on-Demand Catalogue SEO

Print-on-demand stores break most general e-commerce SEO advice, because the advice assumes each product is distinct. In a POD catalogue one design can become forty products, the photography is a mockup shared with thousands of other sellers, and the supplier description is identical across every store using that supplier. Nothing about that is dishonest, but it does mean the default output of a POD workflow is a large catalogue of near-identical pages.

This file is about what to do instead. It assumes you cannot control the base product or the mockup generator, which is usually the reality.

## Understand the duplication you are starting with

Three layers of duplication exist before you write a word.

**Between your own products.** The same design on a t-shirt, hoodie, mug and poster produces four pages whose text differs only in the noun.

**Between your variations.** Twelve colours and five sizes multiply into pages or URLs that are, to a search engine, the same page repeated.

**Between you and every other seller.** The supplier's product description, size chart and care instructions appear word for word on hundreds of stores.

Only the third one is often noticed, and the first two usually do more damage.

## Choose what to build pages around

The most useful decision in POD SEO is picking the right unit for a page. Two structures work.

**Design-led.** One page per design, with the product type as a variation. This suits stores where people search for the theme, joke, fandom or artwork rather than the garment. It produces fewer, stronger pages.

**Product-led.** One page per product type within a design, for example "gardening slogan mug" and "gardening slogan t-shirt" as separate pages. This only earns its keep when people genuinely search for that combination and you are willing to write real copy for each.

Pick one and apply it consistently. The failure mode is doing both by accident: forty auto-generated pages plus a design page, all competing with each other, none of them written for a buyer.

## Making variations behave

Colours and sizes should live on one page unless a specific variant has its own demand. Where a variant does deserve its own page, it needs its own copy, its own images and a reason to exist beyond the dropdown value.

Set the canonical to the page you want ranked, keep sort and filter combinations out of the crawl, and make sure the price shown in structured data matches the price the page displays by default rather than the cheapest variant. This last one is a specific and common POD failure, because the price often varies by size.

## Writing product copy at scale without duplication

You are not going to hand-write two thousand unique descriptions, and pretending otherwise leads to nothing being written at all. A workable compromise:

**Write one genuinely original opening paragraph per design.** Two to four sentences about what the design says, who it appeals to, when someone would buy it, and why you made it. This is the part no competitor can copy, because it is about your design rather than the blank product.

**Standardise the specification block.** Materials, fit, sizing, print method, care. Reuse this freely across products of the same base type. It is factual and repeating it is fine.

**Rewrite the supplier text once, in your own words.** Do it properly for each base product type, not each product. Ten well-written base descriptions cover an entire catalogue.

**Vary the practical detail.** Whether the design sits high on the chest, how the colour reads in daylight, which mug size suits filter coffee. Small true details differentiate a page and help someone buy.

## Titles that reflect real searches

POD titles drift into two failure modes: the machine title, "Design 4471 Unisex Heavy Cotton Tee", and the keyword pile, "Funny Gardening Shirt Gift for Gardeners Plant Lover Mom Dad Tee".

Write for the search a person types, then stop. Lead with what the design is about, then the product type, then the qualifier that matters most for choosing, such as fit or size. Keep gift framing to one clear phrase if gifting is the real use case. Uniqueness matters more than length: if two hundred of your titles differ by one word, none of them stands out.

## Category structure carries more weight than usual

Because individual POD products are weak pages, the collection pages do the heavy lifting. Build categories around how people actually shop: occasion, recipient, theme, style, product type. Give each one a short, honest introduction that helps a visitor choose, and link from it to the designs that best represent it.

A well-written "gifts for new teachers" collection with fifteen relevant designs will out-earn fifteen individual product pages competing for the same phrase.

## Images

The mockup you share with every other seller cannot be your only asset. At minimum, add one image per design that shows scale, context or the print detail. Name files after the design and product rather than the generator's output, and write alt text that describes the design, not just the garment. There is more detail on this in the image optimization guide in this repository.

## Thin content, honestly assessed

A page with a mockup, a supplier paragraph and a size chart is thin, whatever anyone tells you. The test I use: if a customer read only this page, could they decide to buy without opening another tab? If not, either improve it or accept it is a variation rather than a page that deserves indexing.

For large catalogues it is entirely reasonable to have a smaller number of indexable, well-written pages and let the rest exist for people who arrive from your own navigation or from ads.

## Publishing rhythm that works

1. Publish the design page with real copy on day one. Never publish an empty shell intending to fill it later.
2. Add the extra product types as variations, or as separate pages only where demand justifies it.
3. Put every new design into at least one collection and link it from there.
4. Review after four weeks: if the page has impressions but no clicks, the title and image are the problem; if it has neither, the demand or the internal linking is the problem.

## What to stop doing

Stop uploading in bulk with untouched supplier text. Stop creating a page per colour. Stop adding a new design to no collection. Stop measuring the catalogue as a whole; measure your top fifty pages, because they are where a change is visible.

Everything above is what has worked on catalogues I have run or advised on. Verify anything platform-specific against current official documentation before you rely on it.
