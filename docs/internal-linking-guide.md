# Internal Linking Guide for Stores

Internal linking is the cheapest SEO work available to a store owner. You already own every page involved, nobody has to approve anything, and the effect compounds. It is also the work most often skipped, because it is unglamorous and there is no plugin that does it properly.

## What internal links actually do

Three things, and it helps to keep them separate in your head.

They help discovery. A page that nothing links to is a page a crawler has to find some other way, usually the sitemap, and it will be treated as unimportant.

They pass authority. Whatever external links your store has earned flows through your internal links. If every page links to your returns policy and nothing links to your best category, you have decided that the returns policy is your most important page.

They tell the search engine what the target page is about, through the anchor text and the context around the link. This is the part most stores waste completely by linking with "click here", "read more", or a bare product image.

## The structure that works for most stores

Aim for a shallow, obvious hierarchy: home page, then categories, then subcategories where they are justified by real demand, then products. Every product should be reachable from the home page in three clicks or fewer. If a product needs five clicks and a filter, both customers and crawlers will struggle.

Two supporting layers make a large difference:

**Category copy with links.** A short introduction on the category page that links to the two or three subcategories or bestsellers a buyer usually wants. Written for the buyer, it earns its place. Written for the crawler, it reads like filler and everyone can tell.

**Content that links to commercial pages.** A buying guide, a sizing explainer, a materials comparison. These pages answer questions, attract links naturally, and give you a legitimate place to link to categories and products with descriptive anchor text.

## Anchor text without the awkwardness

Use the words a customer would use for the destination page. If the target is a category of waterproof hiking boots, then "waterproof hiking boots" is the anchor, inside a sentence that would exist even if the link did not.

Three habits to avoid: identical anchor text repeated across dozens of pages, which looks templated because it is; anchors that describe your business rather than the destination, such as linking "our range" to a specific product; and stacking three links in one sentence, which dilutes all of them and annoys the reader.

Vary the anchor naturally between pages. Nobody writing normally uses the exact same four words every time.

## Where the links should come from

Rank your pages by the authority they have earned and by traffic, then check what they currently link to. The typical finding is that a store's most linked page is the home page or a blog post from four years ago, and neither links to anything commercially important. Fixing that single mismatch is often the highest value hour in an internal linking project.

Then work through these placements:

- Category introduction copy linking to subcategories and two or three flagship products.
- Product pages linking to their parent category through a real breadcrumb, not only a menu.
- Product pages linking to genuinely related products, with the product name as the anchor rather than only a carousel image.
- Guides and blog posts linking to the categories and products they discuss.
- Footer links reserved for pages users need, not for keyword targets.

## Related products need judgement

Automatic "you may also like" blocks are better than nothing, but they usually surface whatever shares a tag. A short, hand-picked list on your top fifty products beats an automated list everywhere. Think about the actual decision: a customer looking at a mid-range option wants to see the cheaper and better versions, plus the accessory they will need. That is three links that help the buyer and pass authority to pages you care about.

## Orphans and dead ends

Two problems are worth checking every quarter.

An orphan page is one that no internal link points to. Crawl your site, export the URL list, compare it against your product export, and list everything with zero internal links. New products and seasonal categories become orphans constantly.

A dead end is a page with no useful outward link, most often a product that is out of stock or discontinued. If someone lands there from search, give them somewhere to go: the parent category, the replacement product, or the closest alternative.

## Pagination and filters

Paginated category pages should be crawlable so products can be discovered, but they are not pages you promote. Filter URLs should not be linked in ways that invite crawling unless you have deliberately made a filtered page into a landing page with its own copy. See the technical SEO notes in this repository for how to decide.

## Measuring it

Internal linking rarely produces a dramatic overnight change, which is why people abandon it. Measure it properly instead:

1. Record clicks and impressions for the target pages before you start.
2. Make one round of changes, and note the date.
3. Wait three to four weeks. Do not touch the pages in between.
4. Compare, and note which types of link seemed to move the needle.

Over a year this becomes a real internal dataset about your own store, which is worth more than any general advice, including this file.

## A short worked routine

If you have one hour a month, spend it like this. Fifteen minutes finding new orphan pages and linking to them from a relevant category or guide. Fifteen minutes on your five best-selling products, checking that each links to a parent category, an alternative and an accessory. Fifteen minutes adding or improving one category introduction. Fifteen minutes recording what you changed. That routine, repeated, outperforms a one-off audit that nobody implements.
