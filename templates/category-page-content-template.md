# Category Page Content Template

Category pages are the most undervalued pages in most stores. They usually attract broader search demand than individual products, they are where a browsing customer decides what kind of thing they want, and they are the pages that pass authority down to products. In most stores they contain a heading, a grid, and nothing else.

This template is for writing a category page that helps someone choose.

## What a good category page does

It helps a visitor narrow down. That is the whole job. Someone arriving on "waterproof walking boots" does not yet know which pair they want; they want to understand the choice. If your page answers that, the customer goes deeper into the store instead of back to the results.

## Outline

### 1. Category heading (H1)

The name people use, not the internal taxonomy name. "Walking boots" rather than "Footwear > Outdoor > Boots".

### 2. Introduction, two to four sentences, above or immediately below the grid

Answer:

- What this category contains and what it does not.
- How the options differ from each other in the way a buyer cares about.
- What the most common choice is, or who each main option suits.

This is not a place for a keyword paragraph. If a sentence would embarrass you when read aloud to a customer, delete it.

### 3. How to choose, three to five short points

The genuine decision criteria. For boots that might be waterproofing, ankle support, weight and break-in time. For mugs it might be capacity, whether it is dishwasher safe, and print durability.

Each point should be one or two sentences and should link to the subcategory or filtered selection that matches, where one exists.

### 4. Subcategory links with context

Not just a row of tiles. One line each explaining who the subcategory is for, with the subcategory name as the anchor text. This is the internal linking that makes category pages work.

### 5. The grid

Leave the product grid where the theme puts it, but check three things: the default sort makes commercial sense, out-of-stock products are not shown first, and the tile shows the information needed to compare, usually image, name, price and one attribute.

### 6. Longer supporting content, below the grid, only if it earns its place

If you have genuinely useful material such as a sizing explainer, a materials comparison or care advice, put it below the products where it does not push the grid down the page. Keep it short and link to a fuller guide if you have one.

### 7. Questions

Three to five real questions people ask about this category, answered in your own words. Take them from customer emails and chat logs, not from a keyword tool. Answer them plainly, in two or three sentences each.

## Metadata

**Title tag.** The category as people search for it, plus the qualifier that matters, plus the store name if there is room.

**Meta description.** What the category contains and one reason to buy here rather than elsewhere: range, stock, delivery, expertise.

**URL.** Short and stable. Category URLs are the ones you least want to change, because everything below them inherits the change.

## Seasonal and campaign categories

Give them permanent URLs where possible, rather than a new page every year. A page that has existed for four years and gets refreshed annually accumulates value that a new page each year never will. Update the copy, the products and the dates, and keep the URL.

If a seasonal category is genuinely finished, leave it published with an explanation and links to the current equivalent rather than deleting it.

## Empty and thin categories

A category with two products in it is not a category, it is a filter. Merge it, or make it a filtered view of a larger category. Empty categories that remain indexed are one of the quiet quality problems in large stores.

## Before you publish

- Read the introduction as a first-time visitor. Does it help you narrow down?
- Check that every claim about range or stock is currently true.
- Confirm the copy does not repeat, almost word for word, the copy on a sibling category. That is the most common category page failure.
- Check the page links out to at least three specific destinations with descriptive anchor text.
